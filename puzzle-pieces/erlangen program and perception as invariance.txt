> Where does Klein's Erlangen program fit into the system?

● Here's where it lands in the running system.

  Klein's Erlangen program (1872): a geometry is defined by what stays the same under a group of transformations. Euclidean geometry is what is invariant under rotations and
  translations. Projective geometry is what is invariant under projective transformations. The geometry IS the invariance group — you don't define geometry by its objects, you
  define it by what the transformations can't tell apart.

  The paper (gerundelle.tex, line ~110): six areas of mathematics developed independently over more than a century, "from Klein's Erlangen program (1872) to graded monads
  (2014), turn out to describe the same architecture once stated about its own operators and data."

  Perception in Gerundelle IS the Erlangen program applied to a mind. The agent's world — its Umwelt (von Uexküll's term) — is the world simplified by what the agent's
  transforms cannot tell apart. The paper (line ~491): "The agent perceives the fact jar_charged, not a number. The agent's world (its Umwelt) is the world simplified by what
  the agent's transforms cannot tell apart. Evidence is filed into this simplified world, not the raw one."

  The Lean guarantee: fit_factors_through_umwelt — evidence routes through the agent's Umwelt. Every Fit event (an interaction with the world that mints a lived receipt)
  factors through the quotient defined by the agent's invariance group. The agent can't perceive distinctions its transforms don't preserve.

  In the force world (force_world.py), this is concrete: a jar charged to 3 and a jar charged to 5 are both "charged" — the agent's perception maps them to the same gerund
  state. The number is in the world. The state is what the agent perceives. The Umwelt is the quotient: states that the agent's transforms can't distinguish are identified.

  The paper's third claim connects this to affect (line ~116): "Seeing and feeling are one property. What an agent perceives and what it feels are both instances of the same
  mathematical structure: factorization through a view. Perception factors through the Umwelt (the world simplified by the agent's invariances). Affect factors through the
  ownership register (the territory the agent perceives as its own). A theorem proves the connection."

  So:
  - Perception = world factored through the invariance group (the Umwelt). You see what your transforms preserve.
  - Affect = world factored through the ownership register (the territory). You feel what your territory records.
  - Both are factorizations through a view. The same mathematical structure, applied to different registers.

  The paper names four groups of guarantees, and the first group's technical name is "epistemic conservation and invariance" (line ~577): "things that don't change no matter
  what the mind does." Conservation of potential in the world, and invariance of evidence under the agent's transforms, are the base layer — what holds regardless of what
  the mind does with what it perceives.

  The errata (ERRATA.md, item 16) corrects a phrasing: "The Umwelt is the quotient by the agent's group of transforms, not by its actions. Correction: 'what the agent's
  transforms cannot tell apart.'" The distinction matters: the transforms define the geometry, and the geometry defines the perception.

  How it differs from the Erlangen program specifically:

  - Klein's program classifies geometries by their transformation groups. Here, the same idea classifies perceptions by their invariance groups. Each agent has its own group
    of transforms, and its Umwelt is the corresponding quotient. Different agents in the same world perceive different things — not because they have different sensors, but
    because their invariance groups differ. A mind that can't distinguish "charged to 3" from "charged to 5" perceives one state where the world has two.
  - Klein's program is about spatial geometry (Euclidean, affine, projective, topological). Here, the transforms operate on force states, not on spatial configurations. The
    "geometry" is the structure of force-balance states the agent can distinguish, not the structure of space.
  - The bridge to affect is not in Klein. The theorem that perception and affect are instances of the same factorization structure is the paper's own claim — it uses the
    Erlangen idea (factorization through an invariance group) but applies it to two different registers (the Umwelt and the ownership register) and proves they share the
    structure.
