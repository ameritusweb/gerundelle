> Where does the Zeigarnik effect fit into the system?

● Here's where it lands in the running system.

  The Zeigarnik effect (Bluma Zeigarnik, 1927): people remember uncompleted or interrupted tasks better than completed ones. An unfinished task lingers in the mind; once
  completed, it fades. The waiter remembers the unpaid orders and forgets the paid ones. The effect is attentional: the unfinished task keeps pulling for attention until
  closure is reached.

  Open-site tension is the mechanism. Params.T in molecules.py: a constant (default 1.0) added to the force between an open molecule and anything that could fill its sites,
  and added to an incomplete molecule's bid on every tick. The tension does not decay. The concept map states it directly: "An open slot with unfilled binding sites adds a
  constant, non-decaying tension to the molecule's bid every tick. The incomplete thought keeps returning to consciousness not because it's surprising but because it's
  unfinished. That's the earworm mechanism."

  And: "Open-site tension: a constant added to an incomplete molecule's bid on every tick until the site is filled. It doesn't decay. The yearning for the missing piece is
  the pull of an unfilled slot, and it persists until the piece arrives or the thought is parked."

  The three elements of the Zeigarnik effect map precisely:

  1. Uncompleted tasks are remembered better: a molecule with open sites has constant tension in its bid, making it win slots more often. It stays in the pool, stays active,
     stays conscious — because its bid is structurally higher than a completed thought's. The tension term is additive: it doesn't depend on the molecule's activation, its
     surprise, or its novelty. An old, unsurprising, familiar thought with one open site still competes harder than a completed thought with the same activation. Remembered
     better = wins slots more often = stays accessible.

  2. Completed tasks are forgotten: a molecule with no open sites and no remaining surprise (flat precision for Params.dwell = 3 ticks) precipitates into storage
     (Mind.settled). It becomes a box with cooling keys. Completed = no tension, no surprise, settled, cooling. The concept map: "A memory is complete because the thought had
     nothing left to learn." Its keys cool by half-life (Params.h_site = 20 ticks), and eventually it's below Params.cold (0.1) — functionally gone unless a strong resonance
     or a contradiction reopens it. The waiter forgets the paid orders because they have nothing left to learn.

  3. Interruption prevents closure: if a molecule is parked (removed from competition because it was blocked too long — Params.park_after), it doesn't settle into storage
     with closed sites. It's parked with its sites still open. If its source comes back (the interrupted task becomes available again), it re-enters the pool with its tension
     intact. The interruption preserved the openness. This is why the Zeigarnik effect is stronger for interrupted tasks than for tasks that were never started: a started task
     has sites that were opened by engagement; a never-started one has no molecule in the pool at all.

  The fiction ladder showed it. Rung 5: after the toy is taken, its lost slot stays open — pull = 1 + held. The lost toy keeps pulling for attention, tick after tick, until
  either it returns (the slot closes: forgiveness), the keys cool past reach (forgotten: no forgiveness, no access), or the thought is parked (expired open: a grief stored
  with its sites open, ready to reopen on resonance). "Mara remembered how she had played with the toy" — the reopened grief, a Zeigarnik task brought back by resonance.

  The earworm is the degenerate case: a molecule whose open site cannot be filled by anything in the pool or the world. It keeps bidding, keeps winning slots (its tension
  doesn't decay), and nothing can close it. The fiction ladder's rung 0 found this as a failure first: "An unanswerable slot captures a lean mind. Pushing the flag always
  did the same two flips, its conditions slot could never fill, and it pulled forever (95 beats of flag)." The fix: "a hole is paid by the want only while it could be the
  way" — a question that nothing could answer stops pulling. Without that fix, the Zeigarnik effect becomes pathological: the earworm.

  How it differs from the Zeigarnik effect specifically:

  - Zeigarnik's original finding was about recall (uncompleted tasks are recalled more in a list). Here, the effect is about attention, not recall: the open molecule wins
    slots more often, keeping the thought conscious. The recall advantage follows from the attention advantage: a thought that stays conscious longer has more experience
    records, more bonds, more keys, and is easier to retrieve later.
  - The Zeigarnik effect has been difficult to replicate reliably (the meta-analytic evidence is mixed). Here, the mechanism is deterministic: open-site tension is a constant,
    it doesn't decay, and it adds to the bid. The effect's size depends on Params.T relative to the other bid terms (activation, binding score, novelty, support). If T is
    small relative to the pool's activity, the effect is weak. The replication question becomes a parameter question.
  - Modern accounts (Masicampo & Baumeister, 2011) link the Zeigarnik effect to goal-related processing: it's unmet goals, not just unfinished tasks, that persist. Here,
    a want (tagged "goal" in the pool) is never parked — it's pursued through action. Open-site tension and goal status are separate mechanisms. An unfinished task without a
    goal (idle curiosity) pulls by tension alone. An unfinished task with a goal (a want) pulls by tension and by the territory register's served slots. Both exhibit the
    Zeigarnik effect; the goal-related one pulls harder.
