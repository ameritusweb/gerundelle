> Where does Miller's 7±2 (working memory capacity) fit into the system?

● Here's where it lands in the running system.

  Miller (1956, "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two"): the number of items a person can hold in working memory at once is about 7, give or take 2. Later work
  (Cowan, 2001) revised this to about 4 items in the focus of attention. The capacity is a hard limit — you can't hold more items by trying harder. Chunking (grouping items
  into meaningful units) is how expertise fits more into the same slots: a chess master sees one "position" where a novice sees 25 pieces.

  Params.slots = (7, 5, 4) in molecules.py. The tournament has three levels, with 7 slots at the bottom, 5 in the middle, and 4 at the top. These are literal working-memory
  slots: each level's winners compete for the next level, and the top level (4 slots) is conscious attention. The numbers are Miller's.

  The same bid equation runs at every level (Mind.think, line ~539: for L, k in enumerate(self.p.slots)). The bid is always:

    activation × (1 + binding_score) + open_site_tension + w_pred × forward + support

  What changes between levels is not the equation but three things:

  1. The competitors: level 0 gets the full pool; level 1 gets only level 0's winners; level 2 gets level 1's winners. Each level narrows the field.
  2. The experience records are per-level: self.exp[L] holds the Dirichlet counts for pairs at level L. A pair that co-won at level 0 has a different record from the same
     pair at level 1. The concept map: "the same molecule may resolve to different partners at different tournament levels because each level's experience records differ."
     A molecule that wins level 0 by binding to partner A might win level 1 by binding to partner B, because the level-1 Dirichlet for (molecule, B) is stronger.
  3. The number of slots: 7, then 5, then 4.

  So the levels are not different mechanisms — they are the same competition, run on a narrowing field, with level-specific evidence. The narrowing is what makes the top
  level selective: only what survived two rounds of the same competition reaches conscious attention.

  The scarcity is structural — the tournament cannot expand its slots. The concept map: "The tournament has scarce slots (7 → 5 → 4). Thousands of molecules compete; a
  handful win. What doesn't win fades." The slots are what makes attention scarce, which is what makes the competition non-trivial, which is what makes the allocation
  meaningful.

  The system also has separate slot tracks (the user, 2026-10-22: "thoughts, thoughts about thinking, and understanding work compete in separate tracks"):

  - Thought slots: (7, 5, 4) — the main tournament
  - Meta slots: (3, 2, 2) — thoughts about thinking (Meta class)
  - SOV slots: (3, 2, 2) — understanding questions (Understanding class)
  - Phase slots: (2, 1, 1) — emotional phases (Phases class)
  - Experiment slots: (2, 1, 1) — experiments suggested by Inquiry
  - Ownership slots: (2, 1, 1) — territory register instances

  Each track's molecules compete only with each other for their own slots. A meta molecule cannot crowd out a regular thought; an emotional phase cannot crowd out an
  understanding question. This is a structural answer to how a mind can think, feel, reflect, and investigate at the same time without one faculty drowning the others.

  Chunking maps onto composite molecules. When two molecules that resolved to each other as partners both win a level, and a bond molecule acts as an edge between them, the
  three form a composite (Mind.think, rec["formed"]). The composite is one molecule with the parts of its constituents — a chunk. It enters the pool as one competitor. A chess
  master's "position" is a composite: many pieces bonded into one structure that occupies one slot instead of many. The concept map: "A bond is a recognition molecule with two
  binding sites, holding two thoughts together. It can act silently as a structural edge or, when examined, become a conscious thought in its own right."

  The fiction ladder showed the capacity constraint in action. The lean mind's inventory fits in the slot budget: want, condition, push, object, ownership slots. Gerundelle's
  richer pool (many more molecules from its force world) means more competition for the same number of slots — which is why Gerundelle explores more (more molecules bidding,
  more ties, more things tried) and takes longer to converge (the condition molecule has to outbid more competitors to stay at the top long enough to settle).

  How it differs from Miller's 7±2 specifically:

  - Miller's number is about items in short-term memory. Here, the 7 is the number of molecules that can win level-0 slots in a single tick. The tournament runs every tick,
    so the "items in working memory" are the current tick's winners — a snapshot, not a buffer that fills and empties.
  - Cowan's 4 is the focus of attention. Here, Params.slots[-1] = 4 is the top level — the conscious focus. The match is exact.
  - Miller's chunking is about encoding — grouping items before they enter working memory. Here, chunking is structural: composites form in the pool from molecules that bind
    and co-win, and the composite competes as one entity at the next level. The chunk isn't an encoding trick — it's a structural bond that arose from the molecules' own
    interactions.
  - The separate tracks (meta, SOV, phase, experiment, ownership) have no analogue in the Miller/Cowan literature. They're a structural claim about how the mind allocates its
    limited capacity across functions: thinking, reflecting, understanding, feeling, investigating, and owning are all limited, and limited separately.
