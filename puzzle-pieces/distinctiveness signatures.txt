> Where do distinctiveness signatures fit into the system?

● Here's where it lands in the running system.

  The idea (from what_ifs.txt): visual recognition is less about what is common about objects of the same type, and more about the distinctive marks on each individual object
  — a chip, a scratch, a worn patch, a crack in the glaze. You don't need full resolution of the entire scene, only where distinctiveness is expected to reside. And you
  understand the space of valid distinctiveness: marks that came about naturally, not marks that are "uncanny valley."

  This is implemented as distinctiveness space (embodied/DISTINCTIVENESS_SPACE.md). Every real object has marks that make it unlike every other object. Each mark is a molecule
  in the tournament, with the same competition, binding, and decay as any thought molecule. A distinctiveness molecule does four jobs through one mechanism:

  1. Identity — the chip on the rim, once bonded to a cup, brings the rest of its profile with it. One mark, and the mind knows which cup.
  2. Orientation — the chip is to the left of the handle. Rotate the cup: the chip is to the right. The molecules are the same; their arrangement in the gerund field changed.
     The mind reads orientation from where the distinctiveness molecules sit relative to each other, not from an angle measurement.
  3. Gerund tracking — the chip sits in a "resting" region. The cup slides: the chip is now in a "sliding" region. The chip didn't change, its context did. The marks are the
     anchors that carry identity through state changes.
  4. Category recognition — the chip's quality (rough edges, impact-consistent depth, at the rim where cups get knocked) lands in a funded region of distinctiveness space.
     Recognition is: do the distinctive marks on this object, which I have never seen before, land in funded regions? Are they the KIND of distinctiveness that cups have? If
     yes, it is a cup — even though every specific mark is novel.

  Distinctiveness space has structure and density. Funded regions carry density: how commonly marks of this kind occur in the mind's experience.

  - Dense core: common marks (eyebrows roughly there, a handle on the side). Low discriminative value. The mind doesn't need high resolution here. The norm absorbs them.
  - Sparse periphery: rare marks (this specific scar, this unusual wear). High discriminative value. Resolution pools here.

  Unfunded regions are where the uncanny valley lives:

  - Too regular: pores evenly spaced, scratches uniformly distributed. Procedurally generated, not caused. Marks with impossible statistics — a density profile no real
    population produces.
  - Too clean: edges too sharp for a chip, surfaces too smooth between features. Rendered, not broken.
  - Uncorrelated with forces: marks that don't follow grain, wear that doesn't follow use. Generated by a noise function, not by physics.

  The uncanny valley is not about similarity to humans. It is about funded conditions on distinctiveness being contradicted. A CGI face fills every distinctiveness slot — it
  has eyes, pores, wrinkles. But the fillers land in unfunded regions. The conflation detector fires: some bonds say real, some say not. The mind hasn't split yet. It knows
  something is wrong before it knows what.

  The optimization is compression, not discrimination: what is the minimum resolution that still tells this object apart from every other object the mind has encountered?
  More experience makes profiles cheaper, not richer — common marks stop winning tournaments (they're no longer surprising against the population), resolution drops on them.
  Rare marks keep winning. Resolution stays on them.

  The concept map (far right column): "Distinctiveness space: recognition is about whether novel marks make sense, not whether they match a template. A cup you've never seen
  has its own chip, its own handle, its own glaze. You recognize it because the distinctiveness lands in a funded region — the kinds of marks real cups have, even though the
  specific marks are new."
