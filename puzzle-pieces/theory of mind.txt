> Where does theory of mind fit into the system?

● Here's where it lands in the running system.

  Theory of mind (Premack & Woodruff, 1978; Baron-Cohen, Leslie & Frith, 1985): the ability to attribute mental states — beliefs, desires, intentions — to other agents, and
  to understand that others' mental states may differ from one's own. A child who passes the false-belief test (Sally-Anne) understands that Sally will look for her marble
  where she left it, not where Anne moved it, because Sally doesn't know about the move. The child represents Sally's belief as different from reality.

  Several mechanisms in the running system implement aspects of theory of mind, each measured in the fiction or chess ladders:

  1. Perceiving another's wants. In the fiction ladder's rung 3, Leo wants the toy inside the box. Mara asks Leo for help. The lie mechanism (fiction LADDER.md, rung 3):
     "Asked by a mind that wants what it wants (a contested want: a true answer would hand the region to the other), a teller Attests a counterfeit way." For Leo to lie,
     he must represent that Mara wants the toy — that her want is the same as his. This is not a separate theory-of-mind module. It's the territory register: Leo's ownership
     slots show Mara's claim on the same region. A contested slot means two agents want the same thing. The lie follows from the structure.

  2. Attributing beliefs to others. The fiction ladder's rung 3 dramatic irony: "But Leo knew that the latch kept the lid shut." The narrator can tell the reader what Leo
     holds (his understanding register's funded conditions) while Mara's register has that slot open. The reader's ledger holds a truth the character's does not — the irony
     span (16 sentences) is measured as the gap between the reveal and the character's realization. The system represents two minds with different funded slots, and the
     difference is what makes the lie, the irony, and the eventual realization possible.

  3. Trust as a computed quantity. The fiction ladder's rung 3: after a told way fails (Mara presses the lid gently, as Leo said; it doesn't move), "the told receipt is
     retracted, the teller's reliability record is marked down." Trust = (confirmed + KAPPA/2) / (told + KAPPA), updated by each interaction. A second telling from the
     same teller is discounted by this reliability. A teller whose word keeps failing approaches zero reliability.

  4. Recognizing that a lie was a lie. Rung 3: "Mara realized that Leo had lied." Her own tries later give the real condition (the latch holds the lid shut). The learn beat
     compares what she discovered with what Leo told her — a realization that matches what the liar held exposes him. The detection is structural: her funded condition
     contradicts Leo's told way, and the told way came from Leo.

  5. Perceiving another's actions and attributing outcomes. The chess ladder's rung 3 (two minds on one board): each mind perceives what changed since its own last look and
     attributes it to whoever acted (a mover is visible). What the other mind did in between — its experiments, its sub-wants, its planning — is not seen. Only the visible
     act and its consequences reach the perceiving mind.

  6. A helper's act is recognized. The fiction ladder's rung 6: "Mara thanked Ben." A want met by a non-rival's act (the wanted change by someone whose want is not contested)
     is met, not taken: gratitude forms. A want met by a rival's act (the wanted change by someone who wants the same thing) is taken: sadness and anger at the taker. The
     attribution of help vs taking depends on the perceived want structure — whether the other's want is contested or not.

  The chess ladder's rung 3 showed the limits: "Why Black doesn't defend: likely its want." Black's want (standing_black_up) is met only by winning material, and taking back
  after a capture only restores even — nothing pays for defence. Black meets its want only on White's mistakes. Each mind acts on its own evidence and wants; neither models
  the other's strategy. White adapts to the opponent it HAS (empirically: this Black doesn't retake), not to the opponent it INFERS.

  How it differs from theory of mind specifically:

  - Theory of mind is typically described as a module or capacity: either you have it or you don't (Baron-Cohen's "mindblindness" model of autism). Here, the components are
    separable mechanisms: perceiving contested wants, computing trust, detecting lies by comparing registers, attributing outcomes. A mind could have some and not others.
  - The false-belief test requires representing that another agent's belief differs from reality. Here, each mind has its own understanding register with its own funded slots.
    The registers are not shared — Mara's register and Leo's register are separate data structures. The "representation" of another's belief is just the fact that the
    other register exists and can be read (by the narrator, for irony; by the asker, for Pose/Attest). The difference between registers IS the false belief.
  - Theory of mind is typically studied as the ability to INFER another's mental state. Here, inference is limited: a mind sees another's ACTIONS and their consequences, not
    their beliefs, conditions, or plans. The lie is detected not by inferring Leo's mental state but by the world contradicting his word. Mara doesn't figure out that Leo is
    lying; she discovers the truth herself and the contradiction between what she found and what Leo said is the exposure. The theory-of-mind work is done by the world's
    feedback, not by mentalizing.
