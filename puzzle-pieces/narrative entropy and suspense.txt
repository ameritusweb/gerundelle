> Where does information-theoretic suspense (Ely, Frankel & Kamenica) fit into the system?

● Here's where it lands in the running system.

  Ely, Frankel & Kamenica (2015, "Suspense and Surprise", Journal of Political Economy): suspense is the expected variance of the audience's next belief update — how much the
  audience expects its beliefs to change. A Bayesian audience whose beliefs are a martingale feels suspense proportional to E[(μ_{t+1} − μ_t)²]. Surprise is the realized
  magnitude of the update. Suspense is prospective (before the reveal); surprise is retrospective (after it).

  The fiction ladder's rung 7 implemented a multi-endpoint reader model (rung7.py) that tracks the reader's distribution over endings, sentence by sentence:

  - The reader: an open variable over endings. At each sentence, the stories consistent with the text so far (a trie over beat sequences) give a distribution over endings,
    weighted by a uniform cast prior and the reader's expectation of authored events (EVENT 0.08 per available move per round, a genre prior).
  - Entropy curve: the entropy of that distribution at each sentence. Suspense is entropy held late — uncertainty that persists deep into the story.
  - The author's score: value = held × max(rise, 0). Held = entropy weighted by position (i/N): uncertainty kept late in the story. Rise = P(the ending that happens) just
    before it is certain, minus its prior: from unlikely to inevitable.

  The measured results (fiction LADDER.md, rung 7), the chosen story (cast Mara 2, Leo 4; no author moves; value 1.023):

  | sentence                          | reader distribution after it                                        |
  | 1-8 (frame, want)                 | help .53, outlast .22, behind .20, outlasted .05                     |
  | "Mara grew tired of the latch."   | help .49, outlasted .43, behind .09, outlast 0                       |
  | "Leo grew tired of the latch."    | outlasted .55→.77, help .28→.15                                      |
  | "Mara pushed the latch hard."     | outlasted .92, help .08                                              |
  | "Mara gave up, resigned."         | outlasted 1.00                                                       |

  The endings close from the outside in, by themselves: the least likely ending falls first (outlast, when Mara tires), then behind, then help, then the close. Nothing orders
  them; the order is the reader's distribution read sentence by sentence.

  The prior-art report (formal_narrative.md) positions this against the literature: "A reviewer will expect Ely et al. to be cited for the probability-trajectory view of
  suspense, and the paper should state precisely how 'suspense held late × rise of the ending's probability' relates to E[(μ_{t+1}−μ_t)²]: Ely et al. model a Bayesian
  audience whose beliefs are a martingale, so the ending's probability cannot 'rise' in expectation; the paper's score evaluates a realized trajectory."

  How it differs from Ely et al. specifically:

  - Ely et al.'s audience is a Bayesian agent whose beliefs are a martingale — the expected belief tomorrow equals today's belief. The score is the expected variance of the
    next update. Here, the reader model is a trie over beat sequences: it knows every story the author could have told (it has simulated the tree of 350 stories) and tracks
    which endings are consistent with the text so far. The beliefs are not a martingale — they shift as the text reveals information. The score evaluates the realized
    trajectory, not the expected next update.
  - Ely et al. define suspense and surprise as separate quantities (prospective vs retrospective). Here, the two are combined in one score: value = held × max(rise, 0). Held
    is the entropy trajectory (suspense-like: how much uncertainty persists). Rise is the ending's probability change (surprise-like: how unlikely the ending was before it
    became certain). A story with high held and high rise has both suspense and surprise. A story with high held but negative rise (the ending was likely all along, then grew
    less likely before it happened) scores zero — a fizzle.
  - Ely et al. don't model what generates the signal the audience tracks. Here, the signal comes from simulated minds: Mara and Leo act in a world, their reserves drain, their
    choices are determined by their understanding and their wants. The reader's distribution shifts because the characters' internal states (which the reader can't see) produce
    visible actions (which the reader can see). The information asymmetry between the reader and the characters is structural: the reader doesn't know the cast (Mara's reserve
    is 2 or 4), but sees the signs (tiredness, when reserves pass half).
  - Other suspense models in the literature: Gerrig & Bernardo (1994, escape plans), O'Neill & Riedl (2014, protagonist's best plan), Cheong & Young (2015, maximizing suspense
    from reader-plan counts), Doust & Piwek (2017). All model suspense from the protagonist's options. Here, suspense comes from the reader's open variable over multiple
    endings — which can include the protagonist losing, a helper intervening, or the antagonist prevailing. The reader tracks every ending, not just the protagonist's escape.
