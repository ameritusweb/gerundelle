> Where does option pricing theory fit into the system?

● Here's where it lands in the running system.

  Option pricing (Black-Scholes, 1973; financial options theory): an option is the right but not the obligation to act. Its value comes not from exercising it but from holding
  it — the possibility of exercising later, when you know more. The value depends on volatility (uncertainty about the future), time to expiration, and how far the option is
  from being worth exercising. A core principle: do not exercise early while volatility is high, because the option's time value — the chance that circumstances will change in
  your favor — exceeds the immediate payoff.

  The fiction ladder's rung 7 measured this directly. The setup: Mara wants the box open, Leo keeps it shut, both act, reserves determine who outlasts whom. The author has
  moves available — calling Leo out of the room (away), having Ben open the box (help) — but doesn't have to use them. Four endings arise from the minds and the moves: outlast,
  outlasted, behind, help.

  The measured option values (fiction LADDER.md, rung 7 finding 4):

  - Ben by the window, never acting: option value 0.47 (story value 1.023 with him available vs 0.557 without). He never does anything. His presence is worth almost half the
    story's value.
  - The call from the kitchen, never used: option value 0.31 (1.023 vs 0.715 without).
  - Both together with neither exercised: the best story in 350 makes no author move at all.

  Exercising earlier costs value monotonically: away at round 12 gives value 0.95, round 8 gives 0.92, round 7 gives 0.88. The earlier the option is exercised, the less
  uncertainty it has consumed. "Do not exercise early while volatility is high" — the same principle from financial options, appearing in narrative.

  The mechanism: the reader holds an open variable over endings. Each sentence shifts the reader's probability distribution. An unexercised option keeps more endings alive
  — the reader doesn't know whether Ben will act, so every ending where he might and every ending where he doesn't must be tracked. That uncertainty is the suspense. The
  moment an option is exercised, the endings that required it collapse and the endings it forecloses are eliminated. Uncertainty drops. Suspense drops.

  IMPORTANT_REALIZATIONS.md: "An option held open is worth more than an option exercised early."

  The ablation table (fiction LADDER.md, rung 7):

  | moves on offer | stories | best value | held | rise |
  | away + help    | 350     | 1.023      | 1.18 | 0.86 |
  | help only      | 61      | 0.715      | 0.86 | 0.83 |
  | away only      | 62      | 0.557      | 0.73 | 0.77 |
  | none           | 4       | 0.056      | 0.23 | 0.25 |

  Without options, the minds alone give 4 stories and little suspense (value 0.056-0.198). Each option multiplies the story space and keeps uncertainty alive.

  How it differs from financial option pricing specifically:

  - In finance, the option's value comes from the stochastic price process (Brownian motion, log-normal returns). Here, the "price process" is the reader's belief over endings
    — a distribution updated sentence by sentence from a trie over beat sequences. The volatility is narrative: how many endings are still consistent with what has been told.
  - In finance, the holder decides when to exercise. Here, the author decides — the author's search picks the move sequence that maximizes value (held × max(rise, 0): entropy
    weighted by position, times the ending's probability rise). The reader doesn't choose; the reader tracks.
  - Financial options expire. Here, the option persists until the story ends — but the author's helper is a deus ex machina even when introduced in the first lines (rung 7
    finding 5). Help's rise is at most 0.23: the reader is never moved toward it, because Ben has no visible pull (he is not a mind with visible holes). To earn the help
    ending, Ben must be a mind whose uncertainty shows. An option whose exercise is unearned is a narrative debt, not a financial one.
