> Where does the "forces as world model" idea fit into the system?

● Here's where it lands in the running system.

  The idea (from what_ifs.txt): for a world model, you don't need positions, materials, or distances. Instead, use forces, net forces, and potential forces as a proxy for
  affordances, where positions and distances are inferred from the force structure.

  This is the force world (force_world.py). Every object is a balance of external forces against internal reserves. No coordinates. No materials. No distances. The world is a
  tree of doings, and each doing is a gerund: "charged" (potential held by a spring's grip), "resting" (weight balanced by support), "fastened" (a latch's grip exceeding
  the spring's push on a lid). When a force crosses a reserve — when the push exceeds the grip — the gerund flips. That flip is an event.

  The what_ifs.txt entry listed advantages: simpler underlying model (fewer assumptions baked in), scalability with complexity, natural manifestation of physical laws,
  affordance representation, robustness to partial observations. Every one of these is demonstrated in the running system:

  - Simpler: the force world has no Cartesian coordinates, no material properties, no spatial metrics. The fiction ladder's rung 6 showed a stone arch and a steel beam share
    one skeleton — strip() removes names from a gerund tree, leaving typed slots. Material doesn't appear.
  - Scalability: one mind and one algebra run in story, code, chess, and civilization worlds with no domain-specific machinery. The paper: "The architecture is domain-
    independent... The domains are projections, not separate systems."
  - Physical laws: conservation of potential is the world's one law (proved in Lean). A force world conserves its quantity: what one object loses, another gains or the world
    stores. No energy appears from nothing.
  - Affordances: an open force slot (a button to be pressed, a lever with no one at its arm) is what Gibson called an affordance. The invitations the world offers are the
    force slots waiting to be filled.
  - Robustness to partial observations: the mind perceives gerund states and force events, not positions. A hidden object (a toy inside a closed box) is hidden because its
    gerund is enclosed, not because it's behind something. A flag swinging is a visible proxy for the wind's force — the mind doesn't perceive the wind directly, only the
    flag's gerund change.

  The what_ifs.txt entry also listed challenges. Where they stand:

  - Inverting force to infer geometric structure: not needed. The architecture doesn't infer positions from forces — it operates without positions. In the chess world
    (chessworld.py), a piece's state is safe/attacked/hanging/captured, not its square. Rung 0's design choice: "a state is what the rules make of a piece, not where it
    stands." A piece's square is not a fact in this world.
  - Initial and boundary conditions: the world is given as a tree of objects in gerund states with reserves and forces. There are no coordinates to bootstrap.
  - Computational complexity: the force world is cheap because it tracks forces and reserves, not pairwise distances. A civilization with hundreds of agents runs in the same
    framework.
  - Non-physical forces: the 15 relation forms (RELATION_TYPES.md) extend the force vocabulary beyond physical forces. Possession transfer (GO, possessional), status transfer
    (GO, social), communication (GO, mental — content moves between minds), opposition (force, social), competition/ranking (force, social scalar). The same force-balance
    structure covers social and cognitive dynamics.

  The concept map (center-left): "How to represent the world and its relations tractably. Gerund ASTs: every node is a gerund with typed force slots, not a noun with
  properties. A stone arch and a steel beam share one skeleton. Material doesn't appear in the representation; only what things are doing under forces."
