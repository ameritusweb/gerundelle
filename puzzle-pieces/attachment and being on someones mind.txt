> Where does attachment theory fit into the system?

● Here's where it lands in the running system.

  Attachment theory (Bowlby, 1969; Ainsworth, 1978): infants form emotional bonds with caregivers that serve as a "secure base" for exploration. The quality of the attachment
  (secure, anxious, avoidant) shapes the child's internal working model of relationships. Loss of the attachment figure produces a predictable sequence: protest, despair,
  detachment. The attachment bond is not about physical proximity — it's about the mental representation of the other person as available and responsive.

  Being on someone's mind is territorial ownership. The concept map (far right column): "An agent acts to hold and extend the boundaries it perceives as its own. Being on
  someone's mind means occupying a slot in their territory — an investment they maintain. When that slot is lost (forgotten, attributed to someone else), the pull of the loss
  drives behavior."

  And: "How being on someone's mind is an example of territorial ownership and is the result of motivation. An agent acts to hold and extend the boundaries it perceives as its
  own. Being on someone's mind means occupying a slot in their territory — an investment they maintain. When that slot is lost (forgotten, attributed to someone else), the
  pull of the loss drives behavior."

  The concept map on popularity and being understood: "Recognition, appreciation, and attribution are separable conditions: someone can remember you without crediting you, or
  credit you without valuing what you did. Each loss opens a different kind of slot."

  The fiction ladder measured the attachment cycle through rung 5's loss and recovery:

  - Attachment as accumulation: each time Mara plays with the toy, her held count increases. The attachment's strength is pull = 1 + held. One play: pull 2. Four plays: pull 5.
    The attachment is the accumulated investment, not a single bond formed at first contact.
  - Loss and protest: when Leo takes the toy, the held slot transitions to lost. Sadness forms with intensity proportional to the pull. "Mara was heartbroken" (pull 5).
    Anger at the taker: "She grew angry with Leo." The lost slot pays for "what took it?" — she pushes the lid again, searching for the toy where it came from (protest:
    seeking the lost object). Not designed; read off the register.
  - Despair and detachment: without resolution, the phase expires (half-life Params.phase_h). The source is gone (the toy, parked). The phase is stored open — a grief, a
    grudge. Keys cool by Params.h_site (20 ticks). After enough time, the keys fade below Params.cold (0.1). The grief is sealed, inaccessible. Detachment: the mind no
    longer searches, not because it chose to stop but because the access path cooled.
  - Reunion and recovery: the toy returns. If the grief's keys are still warm (resonance: enough keys match among current winners), the box reopens. "Mara remembered how she
    had played with the toy." The grudge reopens too: "She grew angry with Leo again." Reunion restores the full emotional state. If the anger's goal is met (the toy is held
    again), the grudge closes: forgiveness. If the keys have cooled past reach: reunion brings delight at the toy, but no anger at Leo — forgotten, not forgiven.

  The four measured endings (fiction LADDER.md, rung 5):

  | held 4, days   | heartbroken             | remembers, angry again, forgives |
  | held 1, days   | sad                     | remembers, angry again, forgives |
  | held 4, months | heartbroken             | keys cold: no memory, no grudge, delight |
  | no storage     | heartbroken             | nothing stored, nothing reopens: delight |

  Bowlby's internal working model maps onto the territory register's structure of held, contested, and lost slots. A secure attachment is a held slot with a high claim count
  and no contested history — the other person is on your mind, and the holding is undisturbed. An anxious attachment would be a slot that oscillates between held and contested
  — the claim keeps being challenged, and each challenge drains resolve. An avoidant attachment would be a slot that was held, lost, the grief cooled, and the mind no longer
  invests — the access path is cold, and reunion brings no reopening.

  How it differs from attachment theory specifically:

  - Bowlby's attachment is specific to infant-caregiver relationships and evolved for survival. Here, the mechanism is general: any held slot with accumulated claims produces
    the same attachment dynamics — grief proportional to investment, protest as searching for what was held, detachment as cooling. The toy in rung 5 is not a caregiver, but
    the attachment dynamics are the same.
  - Ainsworth's classification (secure, anxious, avoidant) is typological — each child falls into a category. Here, the "type" is continuous: the claim count, the contest
    history, the cooling rate, the resonance threshold. Different temperaments (Params: patience, persistence, curiosity, credulity, stubbornness) produce different attachment
    dynamics from the same algebra. The types are regions of a parameter space, not discrete categories.
  - Bowlby's protest-despair-detachment sequence is described developmentally. Here, each phase has a specific mechanism: protest = the lost slot paying for search; despair =
    the phase expiring without resolution; detachment = keys cooling below resonance threshold. The sequence is not a description but a chain of structural transitions with
    measurable timings (phase half-life, key half-life, cold threshold).
  - Attachment theory doesn't account for forgiveness as a structural closure. Here, forgiveness is the grudge's goal met while the keys are warm — not a decision, not a
    virtue, a mechanism.
